HQ marketing wants Japanese content out at the same pace as every other region: LinkedIn posts, nurture emails, event decks. Nobody at HQ reads Japanese, so every piece routes through the one bilingual person in Tokyo, often the country manager or the only local marketer.

That person becomes the queue. And the problem is usually not their speed. It is that the standard for "good enough to publish in Japan" exists only in their head. The same thing happens on the sales side, when every Japan proposal or stage change waits for one manager.

I ran into this in my own company before I recognised it in clients. When I first handed our LinkedIn company page to a staff member, I was making four to six rounds of corrections per post: two or three on the text, two or three more on the image. Today I look at the posts once a month. My case was a single-language team in Tokyo, not an HQ-and-Japan setup, but the mechanics are the same when the reviewer sits between the two.

## A standard that lives in one head turns review into a queue, and the verdict moves with the reviewer's week

If only one person holds the criteria, only one person can approve. The author waits. The reviewer is busy with higher-priority work and returns comments late. Nobody is idle, yet the days pile up.

The less visible cost is inconsistency. What I approved on Monday I sometimes rejected on Thursday, depending on my own week. From the author's side the right answer moved every day, so they started trying to guess what I would say instead of judging the piece.

For a Japan team this compounds. HQ cannot check the Japanese itself, so it cannot tell whether a delay is about quality, about local fit, or simply about one calendar. Asking the author to "use better judgment" changes nothing when the judgment they need has never been written down.

## Write down the comments you keep making, not a description of quality

The fix was plain. I wrote down every comment I had been making out loud in reviews. It came to a 21-item checklist.

The important part is the form. Items like "high quality" or "reader-first" put you straight back where you started, because only the original reviewer can apply them. Each item has to be a question the author can answer yes or no. For example:

- Does the first line avoid greetings and self-introduction?
- Do the first three lines make clear which one role the post is for? If it is for marketing leaders, does it stay on their concerns, or does win rate take over halfway through?
- Between the problem and the fix, is there one concrete change on the ground, or does the argument jump?

Once written down, most of my "something feels off" reactions turned out to fit questions like these. A few did not. Some were simply my preferences, and I dropped them. The rest were calls that resist a fixed rule, such as whether to mention a specific company or a current news story. Those I kept as exceptions: the author asks me when one comes up.

What I would add for an HQ-and-Japan setup: keep the checklist in English and Japanese side by side. HQ can then review the standard itself, which it can read, instead of every piece, which it cannot.

## Give the first pass to a check and the final call to the author

Next I automated the first pass. When the author, the person who drafts the piece, moves a post to "ready for review" in Notion, the post and the checklist go to an AI model, and a verdict, a score and suggested fixes are written back to the same page. The author does not have to start anything; the check rides on a status change they were making anyway. In a Japan team the author might be a local marketer, an agency or a translator. Whoever drafts it is who gets the final call on routine content.

One rule was fixed from the start: the automated verdict is a first pass. If it scores a post highly and the author still thinks something is off, they fix it. If it scores lower and they judge the post is fine, they publish.

Demanding accuracy from the check would have brought me straight back, reviewing every post to confirm the check was right. The goal was to take me out of routine review, not to install a new reviewer. A colleague working through the checklist can do the same first pass; the tool is not the point.

## The reviewer looks at monthly results, not individual pieces

Four to six rounds per post became one review a month. I did not compare engagement before and after the switch, so I cannot show with numbers that quality held. The author's own effort and growing experience also count for a lot. What I can say is that my time came back, and the place where quality gets checked moved from every post to a monthly review.

In that review we open the posts that worked and the ones that did not, one at a time, rather than scanning a list. The same habit is behind [Run Your Japan Sales Review on One Won Deal and One Lost Deal](/en/articles/review-one-good-one-bad/). What matters here is the next step: when a post missed, fix the checklist rather than the post. Correcting one post helps one post. Correcting one line of the standard helps every post after it.

What surprised me most was not the time saved. The author's language changed. "This kind of opening gets a better response." "This topic drops." I never heard that while I was grading every post. With the standard in my head, the author was looking for my answer. With it written down, they compared and corrected on their own.

## In sales, decide per review type what the first pass may take over

I see the same pattern in sales and revenue operations for Japan teams. Proposals cannot go out until the Japan lead has read them. Whether a deal can move stages is decided out loud in the pipeline call. Whether a CRM record is complete is judged by an admin who reads it and sends it back. None of these reviewers is doing anything wrong. The criteria sit with one person, so only that person can apply them.

Mistakes here cost more than a weak LinkedIn post, so the steps are the same but the hand-off is narrower:

1. **Collect the comments the reviewer has made three or more times recently.** Do not invent new rules; capture the ones already in use.
2. **Turn each into a yes-or-no question that existing fields can answer.** Not "sufficiently documented" but "at the stage change, has the approval-progress field reached the required step?" or "is the customer's internal deadline filled in?" Do not add fields just to run the check.
3. **Have the current reviewer and their manager agree the questions in a meeting you already hold.** A check nobody signed off on just adds a second reviewer.
4. **Split, for each review type, what goes to the first pass and what stays with a person.**

For example:

- **Proposals and quotes:** missing sections and formatting go to the first pass. Pricing, discounts, contract terms and competitor comparisons stay with the Japan lead.
- **Stage changes:** the check lists deals that do not meet the criteria. The manager reviews that list in the weekly pipeline call, not monthly, and the manager, not the rep, decides whether a deal moves anyway, because the forecast depends on it.
- **CRM completeness:** flag gaps or pull them into a list rather than blocking the stage change. Blocking rules can also reject updates from integrations, bulk imports and running workflows. If you do add one, first confirm those can be excluded; how this works depends on the product and configuration, so verify it in yours.

Change cadence differs too. A content checklist can be edited after every monthly review. Sales criteria should collect proposed changes weekly and apply them once a quarter, with the date recorded, so reports from before and after stay comparable.

Deciding which kinds of change need sign-off at all is covered in [Japan content lead time: count the days spent waiting](/en/articles/content-lead-time-count-the-waiting/). That note sets the scope of review; this one takes the criteria out of one person's head. With both in place, the reviewer only sees what actually needs a reviewer.

**When review depends on one person, write down the comments that person keeps repeating as questions anyone can answer yes or no. Hand the first pass to a check or the author, and keep people only on the calls that cannot be written down or that carry real downside.**

If Japanese content or deals are queuing for one reviewer right now, ask that person to write down three comments they have made at least three times this month. Those are the first three lines of a standard they can hand over.
