Your quarterly review shows Japan's pipeline full of stalled deals. HQ asks why the team cannot close.

The local team says "decision-making in Japan is slow," and trust erodes on both sides.

It is a common scene in global SaaS companies operating in Japan.

The problem is not the market. It is the explanation.

## Why "it's the culture" fails

Culture is a black box: it cannot be verified, measured, or improved.

If culture is the cause, there is no action to take, and if there is no action, there is no justification for continued investment.

Explaining Japan through culture quietly builds the case for cutting the Japan budget.

What works is showing the delay as **structure**. Structure can be measured, and what can be measured can be forecast.

## The structure, in three numbers

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published April 2026, n=307, covering company employees and executives who participated with at least one other person in B2B purchases of 3 million yen or more) gives you three defensible numbers.

**1. Multi-step approval is the norm.** 60.9% of companies require two approval stages before an order; over 80% require two or more, and roughly 20% require three or more.

When your champion says yes, the decision has not ended. The internal process has just begun.

**2. Buying takes three to eight months on the buyer's side.** 63.6% of companies take 3 to 8 months from the start of evaluation to vendor selection, and about 40% take six months or longer.

This is the buyer's evaluation period, not your sales cycle. Part of it runs before you are in the deal (see the next number), so measure your own Japan cycle from first meeting before setting forecast dates.

What the number does tell you is that a forecast assuming in-quarter closes for deals still in approval will keep missing.

**3. By first sales contact, the process is 40% done.** On average, the buying process is about 40% complete before serious conversations with a vendor begin.

About 90% of buyers have already shortlisted vendors before contact. Deals entering your pipeline are more mature than they look.

## How to build this into your pipeline

Keep the global stages. Adding a Japan-only stage is the hardest proposal to get through: most global companies share one sales process and forecast categories worldwide, and changing them for Japan alone needs a process branch and HQ RevOps approval.

Instead, add two fields to Japan deals: **the approval level reached** (team, department head, executive, final sign-off) and **the date it entered that level**. This runs as a separate axis from the sales stage, so the global definitions stay untouched. After a few quarters you have days-in-approval by level from your own data.

This reframes the conversation with HQ.

Japan is not slow; the approval step that other markets absorb inside their stages runs long and separately here, and now you can measure it.

Stalled deals become visible as "at department-head approval, day 34" instead of "no update," and the forecast can carry realistic exit dates.

Two practical notes:

- **Do not use the "Japanese deals take 2 to 3 times longer than Western deals" comparison.** It circulates widely, but no published study using the same methodology across regions exists, as far as we can verify. One unsourced number can undermine an otherwise solid deck. Use the Japan-side numbers above, with their source, and let HQ compare against its own benchmarks.
- **Pair the expectation reset with a playbook.** Supporting the champion's internal process (drafting the ringi document with them, preparing a one-page summary for their executives, joining the internal briefing) shortens the time spent at each approval level. Presenting this alongside the data turns the message from "Japan is slow" into "here is how we win in Japan."

**Show HQ the approval steps, the current point of delay and the next condition for progress. Test the explanation against your own deal history rather than attributing it to culture.**

## Common mistakes

- **Explaining with averages only.** "It takes six months on average" invites "make it faster." Show the approval-stage structure so the length has a cause.
- **Sounding like Japanese exceptionalism.** Frame it as "this market has one more measurable step," not "Japan is special." The first fits a management framework; the second sounds like an excuse.
- **Resetting expectations without a plan.** Lowering targets without showing the ringi-support playbook reads as defeatism, not insight.
