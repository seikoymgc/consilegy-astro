In many Japan teams of foreign SaaS companies, one rep carries the business. Often it is the first hire. They know which partner to call, how a buyer's internal approval route works, and when a deal that looks stalled is actually moving.

Then they give notice.

HQ asks them to document their approach during the handover. What comes back is an account list, a schedule of farewell visits to customers, and a page that says "understand the customer's internal situation." Nothing is wrong with it. It just isn't the judgment HQ was hoping to keep.

## Asking for the playbook gets you principles, not judgment

People who perform well rarely put into words what they look at. They don't have to think about it, which is partly why they perform well.

Ask "what's your secret?" and you get "read the buyer's temperature" or "don't miss the timing." True, and useless to the rep who has to decide what to do on Monday.

Japanese survey data suggests this is not unusual. A survey by Thankslab Career (December 2025, 1,014 sales and inside sales staff at mid-sized and smaller Japanese companies with 100 or more employees, reported by MarkeZine) asked how top performers' talk tracks and deal patterns were shared. 46.5% said "partly shared but still dependent on individuals" and 23.8% said "hardly shared at all," about 70% combined. Among the respondents who chose either of those two answers, 59.4% named "know-how depends on individual experience and instinct" as an obstacle, 30.2% said "we don't know how to organize and systematize it," and 27.4% said "top performers are too busy to make time to share."

It measures what reps report, not what works, and it compares nothing outside Japan or between ways of asking. But lack of time and lack of method show up side by side, and leaving it to the rep solves neither.

## Ask what they saw on the day each stage moved

Change the method. Instead of asking the rep to write, someone else opens their deals with them.

Pick three deals the rep won in the last six months, ideally of different sizes or industries. Add one of their deals from the same period that stalled or was lost. What made a deal win is clearest next to one that didn't.

Put the deal's activity history and stage change dates on screen, and for each stage change ask:

- What did you see that made you move it forward that day?
- If that hadn't happened, what would you have done instead?
- What happened that made you contact them on this particular day?

Ask "what did you see," not "why." "Why" pulls the conversation back to principles. "What did you see" brings out observable facts: the buyer's department head said they would join the next meeting; the buyer asked for the quote to be addressed to the president rather than the department.

Six months later, some answers will be reasons added after the fact. For each answer, look together for the activity or email from that day, and keep as a candidate only what the record supports.

About 30 minutes per deal, two hours in total. The interviewer is the Japan sales manager or someone in sales ops who speaks Japanese, and they take the notes. The rep gets no homework, so being busy doesn't stall it. In a two-person office that may be the country manager. HQ RevOps can join, but it cannot run this alone in English, because the details that matter are in how the buyer phrased things in Japanese.

## File the answers in three places

Read the notes back and the judgments usually fall into three kinds, each with its own home.

**Conditions for moving a stage.** For example: "Don't send a quote until a date is set for a meeting that the buyer's manager will attend." Add these to the written exit criteria for the Japan stages. Don't create new required fields for them. A field that isn't known at the time of entry gets filled with a placeholder value, and a Japan-only required field also complicates a global CRM that other regions share.

**Triggers for acting.** For example: "Call in the week the account announces its mid-term business plan." These are outside events the CRM cannot detect, so the rep creates a task from a saved template when they notice one. The manager checks that the tasks exist inside the weekly deal review you already run. If you add a check item there, remove one.

**Conditions for walking away.** For example: "If the buyer postpones twice in a row, stop chasing and set a check date for the next budget cycle." Here the deal goes on hold, not closed-lost, with a check date and an automated reminder for that date, because it is expected to come back when the budget cycle does. When a deal stops for other reasons and is closed as lost, attach a restart condition as described in "[Add a restart condition to lost deals in Japan](/en/articles/reopen-lost-deals-on-a-trigger/)." If walk-away judgment isn't captured, everyone else chases every deal with the same energy.

These examples will differ by deal type. Write down what your own notes contain, in the rep's own words, and keep the Japanese original next to the English so nothing is lost in translation.

## Test it on other reps' deals before it becomes the standard

Not every judgment you capture will work for everyone.

Check first. From other reps' deals over the last two quarters, take ten won and ten lost that reached the stage in question, leaving out losses caused by budget timing. The interviewer reads the activity history and emails and marks each condition as met, not met, or not determinable from the record.

If a condition shows up much more often in wins than in losses, it is a candidate for the standard. If it appears equally in both, it isn't what separates them. Ten of each is a rough check for an obvious difference, not statistics, and in a small Japan team you may have fewer. Conditions that tend to follow from a deal going well, such as the manager joining a meeting, should not be read as causes on their own. If many conditions can't be judged from the record, record them on new deals for a quarter and check then.

Some judgments will turn out to depend on the rep's own relationships. "Call the buyer's executive directly to ask where things stand" only works for someone who has that relationship. Don't make it a standard. Record who holds the relationship with each account as an internal team member on the account record, in whatever form your CRM supports, and decide who inherits it before the rep's last day. For the reassignment itself, see "[Before you reassign Japan accounts, freeze who closed them](/en/articles/reassign-owners-keep-who-closed/)."

Adopt conditions at the start of a quarter, with the agreement of the Japan sales lead, and record the date in the stage definitions. Add them to the exit criteria document only; keep stage names and order as they are, because reordering stages in a shared global pipeline sets off automation and reporting across regions. Use them in weekly deal reviews and change them only at the next quarter.

At the end of the quarter, look at whether other reps' conversion and days-in-stage changed at the stages where you added conditions. The Japan sales lead decides from those numbers whether to keep or drop each one.

## After the resignation, it is usually too late

Once the rep gives notice, the handover period fills up with customer visits and account reassignment.

Do this in normal times, and start with the top rep. Frame it differently, too: not "in case you leave," but "we want your way of running deals to become the team standard." Having their judgment written into the stage definitions is not a bad thing for them. Say up front that this has nothing to do with their performance review. The lost deal is there to show the contrast with the wins, not to score them.

Two options we did not choose.

Using AI to summarize call recordings and extract winning patterns looks cheaper. But a transcript records what was said, and what the rep saw on the day they moved a stage is usually never said aloud. Use the summaries as preparation before the interview. Having junior reps shadow the top rep on every deal does develop the juniors, but the judgment simply moves into another person's head and still isn't held by the company.

**A top rep's judgment is captured one deal at a time, not by asking for their secret. Open their recent wins, ask what they saw on the day each stage moved, file the answers as exit criteria, triggers and walk-away rules, and keep only what still separates wins from losses on other reps' deals.**

Next week, open one recent win with your best Japan rep and ask, "What did you see that day?" The first answer is usually the most interesting one.
