Your Japan marketing lead has resigned. In a small foreign SaaS office in Tokyo, that is often one person, also covering events and PR.

What HQ inherits is a spreadsheet. Programme name, dates, budget, leads. A typical sheet lists, say, a large Tokyo IT trade show, two seminars co-hosted with a local partner, a Japanese whitepaper, a batch of business cards imported after an event. The numbers are right. None of them tells you whether to book the trade show again next year.

So someone tries to reach the person who left. If they are still answering messages, fine. If not, the Japan plan gets rebuilt from global assumptions.

## The spreadsheet records what was done, not what to decide next

In my CRM projects there is a line I hear again and again: "It's faster to ask the previous owner than to read the record." The history is there. The notes are there. People still ask, because the record holds what was said, not how to decide the next step.

A marketing handover fails the same way. A successor needs three answers that a programme list does not contain:

- Why does this programme exist?
- Which customers responded, and to what?
- What happened to those customers after they reached sales?

## Sales and marketing is the hardest role for foreign firms to fill in Japan

JETRO, the Japanese government's trade and investment organisation, surveys foreign-affiliated companies in Japan every year. In the FY2024 edition (fieldwork October to November 2024, published 25 March 2025, 1,427 valid responses from 7,301 companies contacted), 42.4% said they were struggling to secure the people they need (n=1,385). Among those, sales and marketing talent was the hardest category at 57.3% (n=583, multiple response). The sample covers all foreign-affiliated firms, not only software companies.

So the gap between a resignation and the next hire can be long. In that gap, HQ demand gen often ends up owning the Japan budget and reading it against global cost per MQL.

## Without the reasons, HQ makes one of three bad calls

Keep everything. Nobody can explain the programmes, so nobody can justify cutting them. Last year's budget rolls forward.

Cut what you cannot explain. Say the partner seminar looks expensive per lead. What the sheet does not say is that it may have produced the case material Japanese buyers forward to their approvers. Deals can stall two quarters later, and nobody connects the two.

Rebuild from scratch. The global playbook goes in, and the year the previous lead spent learning what works in Japan is spent again.

None of these is a judgment problem at HQ. The information needed for the call was never handed over.

## Keep three lines per programme

| Line | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Why it started | Who started it, and what it was meant to test | To test whether customer case stories land with IT departments at manufacturers. Proposed by the Japan sales head |
| Who responded to what | Which segment responded, to which message or asset. Include who did not | IT departments at companies with 300+ employees took the case booklet. General affairs barely engaged |
| What happened next | Results after sales took the leads, and why | 12 leads handed over, 3 first meetings. Most of the rest said "next fiscal year's budget" |

The first line lets a successor ask whether the question has been answered. If it has, stopping is safe. If not, there is a reason to continue.

The second line matters most when it includes the segments that did not respond. It stops the next person from running the same message at the same audience.

In Japan, that line also carries what HQ cannot see from the dashboard: which assets helped a buyer build the internal approval case, and which events produced business cards from people who will never sit on a decision.

The third line cannot be written by marketing alone; sales has to send results back. In Fast Marketing's 2026 survey of Japanese B2B marketing (May 2026, 432 respondents involved in marketing or sales at B2B companies; a vendor-run survey by a firm that sells B2B marketing support), 82.1% of respondents whose sales teams reported win and loss reasons back to marketing said new deals were above or roughly on target, against 54.3% where sales only reported whether a lead had been contacted. That is correlation, and more mature teams may simply do both. Fixing the return path still pays off well beyond handovers.

## Write the lines as each programme closes, not the week someone resigns

Handover documents get written after the resignation, when the reasons behind a programme from six months ago are already fuzzy and the person leaving is busy. What comes out is the spreadsheet above.

So write the first two lines the week the programme ends: the week after the trade show, or once sales has worked through the seminar leads. Add the third line when sales results come in.

Keep them on the campaign record in the CRM, next to the leads and deals it produced. A successor then sees the numbers and the reasons in one place. A separate tab in a shared sheet is the one nobody opens on handover day.

Written this way, the three lines are simply the programme review.

## If a successor can make one call without asking, the handover works

Pick one recent Japan programme. Ask a colleague to play the successor and decide, from the records alone, whether to keep, change or stop it next year. Contacting the previous owner is not allowed.

If they cannot decide, ask which line was missing. That is where to start.
