A lead count is the number of prospects that meet a counting rule your company has chosen. **If the rule is not written down, the same month produces a different number in every team.** For a global company, the gap shows up when HQ's dashboard and the Japan team's report disagree on how many leads Japan generated.

This note sets out the three lines that decide a lead count, why they drift in Japan, and how HQ and the Japan entity can fix them in one document.

- Lead counts diverge in three places: the unit, the entry point, and the exclusions and cut-off.
- The cost is not an argument about numbers. It is distrust between sales and marketing.
- The fix is a one-page definition sheet, tied to the handoff rule.

I have supported more than 40 CRM and marketing-automation implementations, mostly on HubSpot and Salesforce, and "the lead numbers don't match" is a call I get often, including from global SaaS companies setting up a Japan team.

## Lead counts disagree because three lines sit in different places

A lead count looks like a tally of prospects. In practice it is a decision about what counts as one.

| Line | What you decide | Typical drift |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Unit | Whether one lead is a person, a company, or an inquiry | Marketing counts each inquiry; sales counts each company |
| 2. Entry point | Which touchpoint starts the count | Content download, trade-show business card, webinar attendance, newsletter signup |
| 3. Exclusions and cut-off | What is removed, and when the number is final | Existing customers, competitors, internal contacts, duplicates; month-end or the fifth business day after |

*Figure: the three lines that define a lead count. If any one differs, the same month yields a different number.*

Take a month reported as 300, 180, and 120 leads (illustrative numbers, not actuals). The 300 counts every inquiry from content downloads onward. The 180 merges repeat inquiries from the same person. The 120 also removes existing customers and internal tests, and keeps only contacts sales can actually reach. All three are correct arithmetic. Nobody has decided which one is "the lead count."

Japan adds two sources of drift that HQ definitions rarely anticipate. Business cards collected at trade shows and seminars often enter the CRM as leads in bulk, and company names are written inconsistently, so a company-level count needs matching work first. [Clean the list before you import it into the CRM](/en/articles/clean-lists-before-crm-import/) covers the second problem.

## The cost shows up as distrust, not as a numbers debate

The worst effect of misaligned lines is not the mismatch. It is that nobody can see why the numbers differ, so each side blames the other.

A survey by Softbrain, run by IDEATECH in October 2024 (sales n=159, marketing n=139, 298 in total; a vendor survey with small groups), found that the top complaint from sales about marketing was "lead information is insufficient" at 42.1%, followed by "we don't get the quality and volume of leads we asked for" at 37.1%. In the other direction, 39.6% of marketing respondents said sales does not make effective use of the leads it receives.

Both sides are talking about the same leads, but each is counting something different, so the discussion slides toward emotion.

A survey by Fast Marketing in May 2026 (people involved in marketing or sales at B2B companies, 432 valid responses; read from the publisher's report PDF) supports the pattern:

- 58.1% say they feel a gap between sales and marketing.
- 62.0% say results look fine on paper but do not turn into deals or orders ("often" or "sometimes").
- Goal attainment is 67.8% for lead acquisition, against 57.9% for new orders.

Leads arrive; orders lag. The looser the entry point, the wider that gap gets, because a bigger count does not mean more of those contacts move into the pipeline.

## Data problems start before the lead count does

Lead definitions are not the only issue. HubSpot Japan's annual survey (2024, sales respondents n=1,545, fielded by Macromill; a CRM vendor's own survey, but a published-design series) found that 79% have some difficulty using data, and 24.4% cite weak data sharing with other departments.

When numbers are not aligned across teams, the lead count is the first metric to break. It has the most entry points and the most people touching it.

## Fix the three lines on one page and tie them to the handoff rule

The method is simple. The order matters.

**1. Start with line 3.** Decide what is removed first: existing customers, competitors, internal contacts, duplicates. What remains becomes "a prospect worth contacting." Debate about widening the entry point comes after.

**2. Set line 2 at a touchpoint sales can act on.** Decide whether one download is enough or repeated visits are required. Draw the line where a sales call would actually be a conversation. Combining attributes with buying behavior, such as visiting the pricing page or reading a comparison article, is covered in [Hand off on behavior, not on score](/en/articles/handoff-on-behavior-not-score/).

**3. Fix line 1 as a single unit.** Person or company. In B2B, counting by company usually maps better to deals, but only if company matching is done. If it is not, count people first and report the company count alongside.

**4. Put all three lines on one definition sheet and record approval from each function.** A verbal agreement reverts when people change.

Then decide where the counted leads are handed to sales. In the same Fast Marketing survey (2026, n=432), companies with a clear handoff standard that is actually operated reported an order-attainment rate of 84.6%. Companies with no standard and individual discretion reported 51.9%, and companies with no handoff process at all reported 26.1%. That is a gap of more than 30 points. Writing the lead lines in the same document as the handoff standard makes both harder to drift.

## Changing the definition breaks comparison with the past

Redrawing the lines breaks comparison with earlier numbers. Two steps help. Change definitions only at a quarter boundary, and count one quarter under both the old and new definitions so you leave a conversion factor behind. Record both on the definition sheet's change log. Without this, nobody can explain the year-over-year gap six months later.

## FAQ

### How is a lead count different from an MQL count?

A lead is the entry count of prospects. An MQL is the subset that marketing has judged ready to hand to sales. If the lead lines are not fixed, the MQL conversion rate changes meaning too. See [Your Japan team's conversion rates don't match HQ's? Start with the denominator](/en/articles/define-the-denominator-first/).

### Should leads be counted by person or by company?

In B2B, deals move at the company level, so company is the default. But company counts require matching duplicate company names first. If that is not ready, count people and show the company count next to it.

### Why do lead counts rise while deals do not?

The entry point is probably wide, so people who only downloaded content outnumber contacts worth a sales call. In Fast Marketing's 2026 survey (n=432), 62.0% reported results that look fine on paper but do not become deals. Review the handoff standard before widening the entry point.

### Who should decide the lead definition?

Representatives of marketing, sales, and customer success decide it in the same meeting. Taking it offline lets each function's convenience leak in. Record the decision with everyone's approval on one definition sheet.
