The same Japan deal appears in "closing this month" for the third month running. The rep says the customer is positive. At HQ the question becomes whether the local team is too optimistic.

That question is usually the wrong one. The more useful one is whether anyone can show why the deal was counted.

In one engagement we supported, the account owner knew the buyer was waiting for next fiscal year's budget. She had said so in meetings. The CRM held only a stage and an expected close date, so she pushed the date back a month at a time by hand. The deal was counted as "closing this month" every month and missed every month.

The fact that decided the timing was known to the team and had no place to live in the forecast.

## A miss is only explainable if the day-of basis was kept

Forecasts miss for several reasons. The buyer's budget changes. A competitor enters. An internal decision slips. The rep misreads the situation. All of these happen in Japan as anywhere.

The trouble starts after the miss. To tell the reasons apart, you need the basis that existed on the day the deal was counted: how far the approval had climbed, when the budget was expected to be set, and who held the ball (your team, the buyer's contact, or the buyer's approver). Then you set that next to what changed afterwards.

A CRM normally shows you the latest state. Some tools keep field history, but few companies keep the reason a deal was counted in one place and pull it up in the meeting after the miss.

Without it you cannot separate "the buyer changed course" from "the first read was wrong." When you cannot separate them, the blame lands on the most visible person, the local rep.

Adding fields does not fix this either. If no meeting uses the fields, people stop filling them in. The rep has no reason to enter information nobody reads.

## Treat record, forecast, review and fix as one loop

Revenue architecture, as set out by Jacco van der Kooij of Winning by Design in his 2023 book, treats revenue as one customer process in which recording, forecasting, deciding, verifying and improving have to run together. The company's Bowtie model tracks conversion and time at each stage.

For a Japan forecast, five things need an owner.

| What to decide | What it means |
|---|---|
| Record | Approval stage, expected budget timing, who holds the ball, and the date each one changed |
| Count | Which conditions put a deal into this month's number |
| Review | Check the basis in the forecast meeting, adjust, and keep the basis as it stood that day |
| Compare | For missed deals, put the day-of basis next to what happened since |
| Fix | Change the counting rule or the sales action based on the comparison |

None of these is new. Having them as one loop is what matters. If one is missing, the others stall.

Once the dates of approval changes accumulate, you can see time by approval stage across deals, which is what the Bowtie's stage-by-stage timing is meant to show.

## For Japan, put the buyer's budget and approval calendar into the count

The framework is global. The operating detail is not, because it follows how the buyer buys.

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published April 2026, n=307, limited to buyers who took part with at least one colleague in purchases of 3 million yen or more; a vendor-run survey skewed toward large, multi-stakeholder deals) found that 60.9% of companies run two approval layers. The approvers move the decision forward inside the company without attending your meetings. If you only mark your own activities as milestones, you cannot see that progress.

Budget timing differs by company too, including whether money can be spent mid-year. Rather than apply a generic calendar, confirm three things per account and feed them into the count:

- When, and at which meeting, is the budget decided?
- Whose approval does the deal pass through, and where is it now?
- As of what date was each of these confirmed?

A deal known to wait for next year's budget goes into next period's number, not this month's. The deal above could have moved there at the first meeting where the rep mentioned the budget wait.

The axis that decides which approval rung a deal sits on is covered in [Forecast your Japan pipeline on approval progress, not rep confidence](/en/articles/forecast-by-approval-not-confidence/). This note is about keeping the basis for that count, dated, and setting it against the result.

## What changes when a deal misses

We cannot promise this makes the forecast accurate. Whether a count built on budget timing and approval stage predicts better only shows after you compare forecasts with outcomes a few times.

What changes is the miss itself.

| | Without the day-of basis | With the day-of basis |
|---|---|---|
| The review after a miss | Question the rep's judgment | Lay the day-of basis next to what changed |
| Sales action | Activity targets, manager ride-alongs | Find the approval stage where deals stall and act there |
| Headcount | Long cycles read as too few reps | Check how much of the time is buyer-side approval and budget waiting |

If most of the delay is on the buyer's side, material for the approver and proposals timed before budget setting will do more than another hire.

Waiting is not free. In the 2025 benchmark by Ebsta and Pavilion (n=655,000 deals across 387 companies; non-Japan data), a deal that slipped one month won 13% of the time, and one that slipped three months won 8%. Carrying a missed deal forward with "next month, for sure" weakens it quietly.

## Before your next forecast meeting, open three deals

Open three deals currently "in negotiation" and try to write two things for each:

1. Who holds the ball now: your team, the buyer's contact, or the buyer's approver?
2. Why is the deal counted this month, and as of what date was that information true?

Write "unknown" for anything the record cannot show. Many unknowns mean the fields are missing, the entry has stopped, or no meeting uses the information. Ask whether the basis was kept before you ask about the rep.
