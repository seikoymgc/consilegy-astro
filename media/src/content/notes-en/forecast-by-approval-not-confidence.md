Your Japan pipeline is full of deals at 90%. The local team reports every conversation as positive. Then the quarter closes and half of it moves to next quarter.

The reps are not sandbagging or over-promising. Confidence is being set by their read of a person who cannot approve anything.

Run a second axis next to the sales stage that tracks how far the approval has actually climbed, and forecast on that.

## Positive meetings are not decisions

In a survey by ProFuture and Macromill conducted on 3 and 4 February 2026 (n=103, employees working in B2B marketing roles in Japan; a self-run survey by a B2B marketing services provider with a small sample, so read it as directional), 8 in 10 companies said their marketing budget would hold or rise for the coming year: 48.5% unchanged, 32.0% a slight increase, 2.9% a large increase.

In the same survey, only 9.7% said they were reaching decision-makers adequately.

Budget is up, leads are up, and the person who signs has not been reached. Your CRM says "in negotiation." Nothing has moved inside the account.

## In Japan, the gap between evaluator and approver is structural

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published April 2026, n=307, limited to buyers who participated with at least one colleague in purchases of 3 million yen or more, so it reflects larger multi-stakeholder deals; it is also a vendor-sponsored survey) found that 60.9% of companies require two approval layers and more than 80% require two or more.

The functions involved are information systems at 45.6%, the business unit at 37.5%, and corporate planning at 29.0%.

Your champion's yes is one of four, and headquarters is reading it as the deal.

## Add the second axis

Do not try to calibrate rep confidence. Add an axis.

Define the approval ladder as stages your local team can observe: the working level agrees, the department head approves, the executive approves, the approval is complete. Keep the sales stage exactly as it is.

Take the lower of the two. A deal at 90% sales confidence sitting at "department head approves" is carried at that rung's weight, not at 90%. That single rule stops the higher number from setting the forecast.

Write the exclusion rule down before you need it: a deal that has not reached executive approval is not in this quarter's commit. It stays in the pipeline. It leaves the forecast.

## Stalled deals do not come back

Ebsta and Pavilion's 2024 benchmark (n=4.2 million opportunities; an international study, not Japanese data) found that 44% of deals slip, and slipped deals win 67% less often.

Their 2025 study (n=655,000 opportunities, also international) puts the actual win rate at 13% for deals that slip by a month and 8% for deals that slip by three.

A deal held in the forecast while the approval sits still is not deferred revenue. It is revenue that is quietly going away.

## What headquarters should ask instead

- **Stop asking "what is the confidence on this deal."** Ask whose approval is missing. If the local team cannot name a person, the deal is not positive, it is unobserved.
- **Stop treating a slipped Japanese deal as a coverage problem.** More pipeline does not fix approvals that never climb. Adding leads adds champions, not signatures.
- **Stop forcing the global stage model to carry both jobs.** Sales stages track what your team did. The approval axis tracks what the buyer decided. One field cannot hold both, and merging them is why the Japan number is never right.
