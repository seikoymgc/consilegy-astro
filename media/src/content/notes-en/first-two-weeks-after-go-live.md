The global CRM goes live for the new Japan team. The rollout project closes on schedule, and the team is told to raise a ticket if anything is unclear.

Two weeks pass. No tickets. HQ reads this as a clean launch.

A quarter later, the Japan pipeline review is running out of a spreadsheet, and the CRM shows a handful of half-filled records. Nothing broke loudly. It just never started.

## Why "no questions" means something different in Japan

In most US or EMEA teams, friction produces noise: tickets, Slack threads, complaints in the enablement channel. You can reasonably use silence as a health signal.

In a Japan entity, you mostly cannot.

Raising an issue in a global channel means writing it in English, in public, about a system HQ just paid for. Most members will not do that for a field label that does not fit their deal process. They will work around it quietly, and the workaround becomes the habit.

So the signal you should read is not tickets. It is the data itself: who has logged in, which mandatory fields are being left empty, which stage every Japanese deal is parked in.

Zero questions plus thin data is not adoption. It is your earliest and cheapest warning.

Why two weeks? This is a rule of thumb from the rollouts we have supported, not a survey finding.

Within the first few weeks a team tips toward either a using habit or an avoiding habit, and two weeks is roughly when the tilt becomes visible in the data. Retraining after the avoiding habit has set in means first undoing the shared assumption that this tool, too, will fade.

## Keep the project open for two more weeks

Do not let the rollout team leave at go-live. Three things, in order:

**1. Read Japan's field-level data every day.** Ten minutes each morning: logins, empty fields, stuck stages. Assign this to someone who can read the local context, not just the dashboard.

The data will tell you what the team will not say in a global channel.

Ten minutes is only realistic if the views exist before go-live. Build three: records created and updated per rep, the count of empty values in the key fields, and days spent in each stage.

How you build them depends on your CRM and its configuration. Starting on go-live day means missing the first few days.

**2. Fix frictions within 48 hours.** The most common Japan-side friction is a global mandatory field that assumes a sales process Japan does not run, such as a single decision-maker field in a consensus-driven deal.

Remove it for Japan, shorten the picklist, or move the input into an existing local meeting. Small, fast changes. No change request cycle.

Fast is not the same as unchecked.

A field or picklist value may be referenced by reports, automations, integrations to other systems, and records already entered. Removing a picklist value can strand the existing records that carry it.

Before changing anything, check where the field is used, and prefer hiding it for Japan or blocking it for new entries over deleting it. Log every change in one line: what, when, and why. That log becomes the material for step three.

**3. Close the loop visibly.** At the end of week two, tell the Japan team exactly what was changed because of what the data showed.

Once the team sees that friction gets fixed without anyone having to escalate in English, the reporting starts to flow. Fix things silently and you get credit for none of it, and the silence continues.

Make the ownership explicit before the project closes. The person reading the data is a Japan-side rollout lead who knows the local sales process; the person fixing is a CRM admin with rights to change the configuration.

At the go-live decision meeting, the project sponsor approves moving the project end date to two weeks after go-live and keeping both people's time allocated until then.

## The principle

**Keep ownership of adoption after go-live. Review field-level records during the first two weeks, contact users about missing or inconsistent entries, and verify that fixes reach them.**
