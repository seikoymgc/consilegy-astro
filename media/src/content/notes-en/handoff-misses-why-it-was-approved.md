A Japan account renews late, or not at all, after twelve months of healthy usage and no support escalations. The account team writes "price" in the loss reason, because price is the only field with a plausible value in it.

The handoff record from the original close is complete. It is also missing the only artifact that would have decided this.

## A complete handoff record and a lost renewal are not a contradiction

Look at what a standard closed-won handoff captures: contract terms, billing schedule, named contacts and their titles, go-live date, integration scope, the success criteria as written in your own mutual action plan. All accurate. All reusable. All of it answerable without asking the seller.

That is the tell. Fields get added to a handoff template when someone can reliably fill them, and the things a seller can reliably fill are the things that are already documented somewhere else. The knowledge that exists only in the seller's head does not have a field shape, so when you give it one it comes back as "improve operational efficiency."

In a domestic North American or European deal you can survive this, because the economic buyer who signed is usually still the economic buyer at renewal and can reconstruct the rationale from memory. In Japan that assumption does not hold, and it fails for structural reasons rather than accidental ones.

## The sale ended as a Japanese-language document you have never seen

A Japanese enterprise purchase of any size clears a *ringi*, a written circulating approval. Someone on the customer side authored a document, in Japanese, stating what this spend is for, which budget line it comes from, what will be stopped or not stopped, and why now.

Your seller did not just wait for that document. In a well-run Japan deal the seller helped shape it: which framing survives the finance review, whether to position the purchase as new investment or as a replacement, what the first line has to say for the approver to read the second. The finished justification is written in the customer's own vocabulary and is usually about their internal workload, not about your product. Something closer to "eliminates the three days per quarter currently spent assembling the divisional report" than to anything in your feature list.

That sentence is half your seller's work product. It is also the only version of your value proposition that has ever been read by the people who release the money. And it is nowhere in your CRM, because your CRM has a field for contract value and no field for what the contract was approved as.

## The author of that sentence leaves the seat on purpose, not by accident

Western renewal playbooks assume champion loss is an exception to monitor. In large Japanese companies, planned internal rotation every few years is normal practice, applied to people who are performing well. Your champion is not leaving because they are unhappy. They are leaving because it is their turn.

When they move, the approval document moves with their department, into a shared folder their successor has no reason to open and often does not know exists. The successor inherits a running contract they did not choose, a monthly invoice, and a login.

Then renewal arrives. The successor cannot approve a continuation they cannot explain, so they escalate. The person above asks what this was originally for. Nobody in the room can answer. At that moment your renewal stops being a satisfaction question and becomes a fresh approval cycle, competing for budget against things with sponsors who are present.

No competitor has to show up for you to lose this.

## The room was larger than your CRM ever recorded

IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa of Demagen Research Institute surveyed buyers between 25 and 26 May 2026 (an internet panel survey run under IDEATECH's research-PR service, covering people who were one of two or more participants in the purchase, replacement or major renewal of B2B business software or IT infrastructure with an annual contract value of 5 million yen or more; 330 valid responses, this question n=325). Deals involving seven or more decision participants were 58.5% of the sample. Departments that took part included finance and accounting at 38.2% and legal at 33.2%.

The survey exists to demonstrate the vendor's own research service and it is a panel sample, so read the order of magnitude rather than the exact ratio. The magnitude is the point. Seven or more people, several of them from functions your account executive never met, formed a shared understanding of what this purchase was for. Your CRM stored one or two of their names.

HiCustomer's *Customer Success White Paper 2023* (fieldwork 18 to 30 April 2023, 203 valid responses) found that among companies reporting an inadequate handoff from sales to customer success, 63.3% had a churn rate of 2% or higher, 1.7 times the rate among companies reporting an adequate handoff. Small sample, self-reported, run by a vendor in the customer success tooling market, and correlation rather than cause: companies with low churn are probably better organised overall, handoffs included. Worth looking at once, not worth building a business case on.

## HQ will diagnose this as pricing, because pricing is the only thing measured

Here is how the misreading happens. Usage was fine. No escalations. The relationship felt warm right up to the end. The only variable that visibly differs between your Japan book and your home market is the price point, so the quarterly review concludes that Japan is price sensitive and someone proposes a local discount tier.

The discount will not help, because the customer never got to a price conversation. They got stuck one step earlier, at not being able to state what the thing is for.

The cost of the wrong diagnosis compounds. Discounting to fix a memory problem lowers the value of the next renewal too, and it trains the Japan team to believe that closing is the finish line, which is exactly the belief that produced the empty handoff.

## One line, in the deal record, in both languages

Two changes, and neither requires a new tool.

At close, the seller writes one sentence: what this contract was approved as inside the customer's organisation. In the customer's words, without your product name in it. Attach the name and department of the person who authored the approval, and the department that released the budget. Write it in Japanese first, because that is the language the justification has to survive in, then add an English line for HQ. A translated summary is enough for the second one.

Put that sentence on the deal or opportunity record, not in the onboarding handoff document. The handoff document has done its job once onboarding ends; the deal record is what someone opens twelve months later. One text property is the whole implementation.

Keep the content to three things: the business situation behind the purchase, who decided, and what the customer previously tried that failed. The third is the one that pays. Knowing what already failed lets a renewal conversation start somewhere other than a usage report, which is how these meetings otherwise end up sounding. How to help build that approval document in the first place is in [when sales drafts the internal approval document, stalled deals start moving](/en/articles/draft-the-internal-approval-document/).

**In Japan, the handoff must carry the sentence the customer's internal approval document used, not the facts about the customer. Without it the renewal is not a continuation decision, it is a second approval cycle run by people who were not there the first time.**

Pick three Japan accounts renewing within ninety days. For each one, try to write what the contract was approved as without asking the account executive. Where you cannot, the seller who closed it is probably still reachable this quarter.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding fields to the handoff template instead of adding one sentence.** More fields produce more complete records of things you could already look up, and leave the same gap.
- **Storing the rationale in English only.** The sentence has to work in the room where the renewal is approved, and that room runs in Japanese.
- **Treating champion rotation as churn risk to prevent.** It is scheduled, it is normal, and it is a reason to write things down rather than a relationship to defend harder.
- **Reading a stalled renewal as a price objection.** Ask who on the customer side would have to explain this spend upward, and whether that person was present at the original decision.
