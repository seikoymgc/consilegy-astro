The Japan team reports that the demos go well. The prospect nods through the ROI slide. Then the deal sits for weeks, and the champion comes back with one line: they cannot explain the cost-effectiveness internally.

Headquarters usually hears this as a weak value proposition. **More often the ROI was delivered in the room, and nothing the buyer can carry out of the room contains it.** The person who was convinced is rarely the person who signs. They are the person who has to convince others.

## Buyers get stuck on the ROI estimate before they talk to sales

IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (Demagen Research) surveyed people in Japan in May 2026 (fielded May 25-26, 330 valid responses, n varies by question). Respondents had been involved, with at least one other person, in buying, replacing or renewing B2B software, SaaS or IT infrastructure and security products worth 5 million yen or more a year in the previous 12 months.

Asked what was hard to judge on their own before contacting a vendor, 56.3% named estimating the operating burden after rollout, 47.1% named feature comparison, and 41.5% named ROI estimation. Putting numbers on effort and return was harder for buyers than comparing features.

The content they found most helpful was an ROI calculator, at 55.4%. Case studies from similar industries and sizes followed at 46.5%, slides for internal explanation at 26.8%, and support material for the approval request at 14.2%. The content that did not affect the decision was general industry trend reports (49.5%) and feature comparison tables (46.2%).

The reasonable reading is that buyers do not want the answer you calculated. They want the means to calculate it.

## Price dominates the stated criteria, and ROI sits inside it

In the same survey, the top criterion for choosing a vendor (up to three answers) was price and cost at 58.5%. The persuasiveness of ROI was cited by 11.1%.

I would not read that as ROI mattering little. For most buyers ROI is probably not a separate criterion. It is the work of showing that the price is worth paying, so it appears as price. What decides the deal is whether the buyer can justify the price internally, which means the useful thing is material for justifying, not a better argument.

## A model the buyer owns travels through the approval chain

Approval in Japan involves several readers. In the earlier IDEATECH and Kitagawa survey (April 2026, n=307, respondents involved with others in purchases of 3 million yen or more), over 80% reported two or more layers of approval. Your champion's account of what your rep said reaches the next layer as hearsay.

Compare two sentences. "Sales told us it saves about this much." And: "With our numbers, it comes to this." The second can be questioned and answered by the person who brought it. That difference is what survives a ringi meeting. Giving the buyer what they need to convince everyone else, not just convincing the buyer, is the core of this. An editable ROI model is the clearest form it takes.

## Three rules for building it for Japan

It does not need to be a tool. One spreadsheet is enough. Three rules matter.

Keep inputs to five or fewer, and ask only for numbers the buyer has to hand: monthly hours, headcount, unit cost. If an input is hard to find, add one line saying where to look. A long form will not be finished.

Show the assumptions on screen and let the buyer overwrite them. If you hide the savings rate, the output has the same standing as your rep's claim, and the champion cannot answer "where does this number come from?" in a meeting you are not in. When the buyer can correct an assumption to fit their own business, the result becomes a number they own.

Show three cases, not one: optimistic, standard and conservative. Approval discussions in Japan tend to test the worst case, and a conservative figure your team publishes first is one the champion can use as it stands.

## What the Japan team can do this week

Ask the Japan team to recall three recent deals and find the moment a buyer asked, "What would this come to for us?" If the rep answered by calculating on the spot, that calculation is your material. While it stays in the rep's head, the buyer has nothing to use in a ringi.

Turn it into a sheet with five inputs or fewer. Decide later whether to publish it or hand it over after the second meeting. Building it usually shows that each rep has been using different assumptions, which is worth knowing before HQ sees the pipeline.

If you want to use a real customer's figures, get their permission first. If you cannot, a blank sheet with the assumptions filled in is enough.

One caution on the data. The survey was run through IDEATECH's own survey PR service as an online panel. Read the percentages as orders of magnitude and rankings, and do not mix them with the earlier survey, which used a different amount threshold and different questions.
