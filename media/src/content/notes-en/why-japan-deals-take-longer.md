Your quarterly review shows Japan's pipeline full of stalled deals. HQ asks why the team cannot close. The local team says "decision-making in Japan is slow," and trust erodes on both sides. This scene repeats itself in almost every global SaaS company operating in Japan.

The problem is not the market. It is the explanation.

## Why "it's the culture" fails

Culture is a black box: it cannot be verified, measured, or improved. If culture is the cause, there is no action to take, and if there is no action, there is no justification for continued investment. Explaining Japan through culture quietly builds the case for cutting the Japan budget.

What works is showing the delay as **structure**. Structure can be measured, and what can be measured can be forecast.

## The structure, in three numbers

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published April 2026, n=307, covering company employees and executives who participated with at least one other person in B2B purchases of 3 million yen or more) gives you three defensible numbers.

**1. Multi-step approval is the norm.** 60.9% of companies require two approval stages before an order; over 80% require two or more, and roughly 20% require three or more. When your champion says yes, the decision has not ended. The internal process has just begun.

**2. Three to eight months is the standard evaluation window.** 63.6% of companies take 3 to 8 months from the start of evaluation to vendor selection, and about 40% take six months or longer. A forecast built on in-quarter closes will structurally fail in Japan.

**3. By first sales contact, the process is 40% done.** On average, the buying process is about 40% complete before serious conversations with a vendor begin. About 90% of buyers have already shortlisted vendors before contact. Deals entering your pipeline are more mature than they look.

## How to build this into your pipeline

Add one stage to your sales process: **internal consensus building (ringi)**, between selection and contract. Then measure the waiting time in that stage, separately from your standard sales cycle.

This reframes the conversation with HQ. Japan is not slow; your stage definitions are missing a stage that exists in this market. Once the stage exists in the CRM, stalled deals become visible as "in ringi, day 34" instead of "no update," and the forecast can carry realistic exit dates.

Two practical notes:

- **Do not use the "Japanese deals take 2 to 3 times longer than Western deals" comparison.** It circulates widely, but no published study using the same methodology across regions exists, as far as we can verify. One unsourced number can undermine an otherwise solid deck. Use the Japan-side numbers above, with their source, and let HQ compare against its own benchmarks.
- **Pair the expectation reset with a playbook.** Supporting the champion's internal process (drafting the ringi document with them, preparing a one-page summary for their executives, joining the internal briefing) shortens the invisible stage. Presenting this alongside the data turns the message from "Japan is slow" into "here is how we win in Japan."

## Common mistakes

- **Explaining with averages only.** "It takes six months on average" invites "make it faster." Show the approval-stage structure so the length has a cause.
- **Sounding like Japanese exceptionalism.** Frame it as "this market has one more measurable stage," not "Japan is special." The first fits a management framework; the second sounds like an excuse.
- **Resetting expectations without a plan.** Lowering targets without showing the ringi-support playbook reads as defeatism, not insight.
