When a Japanese prospect asks for a competitive comparison, translating headquarters’ battlecard may leave the most important questions unanswered. A feature can exist while its implementation cost, ownership and operating conditions remain unclear. The buyer needs to assess those conditions before explaining the proposal to colleagues.

Consider a hypothetical requirement: transferring orders to an existing sales management system without entering the same information twice. “Integration available” does not explain who builds the connection, what the quotation covers or who handles failures. These are questions about fit, not simply feature count.

This article proposes a requirements-based comparison for teams selling into Japan. It does not assume that all Japanese buyers follow the same approval process, or that this problem is unique to Japan.

## Japanese survey evidence supports discussing features as well as references

A [2026 survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000629.000045863.html) found that references from similarly sized companies and clear functionality addressing the buyer’s problem were influential when vendors entered consideration: 44.3% and 40.3%, respectively.

The online survey ran on 23–26 March 2026 and covered 307 people involved with colleagues in Japanese B2B purchases costing at least ¥3 million annually or at implementation. This question allowed up to three answers from 298 respondents whose purchase was decided or narrowed to final candidates. It concerned entry into consideration, including a vendor expected to receive the order, rather than the final reason for awarding a contract. The publisher provides survey-based PR; these responses should not be treated as representative of every purchase.

The evidence does not support saying that buyers eliminate vendors on features but never select them on features. Our editorial recommendation is to retain clear feature information and connect it to the buyer’s requirements. A reference is useful context when its conditions are explained, rather than proof that the same result will follow elsewhere.

## Rebuild around the Japanese prospect’s requirements

Start with the work the prospect wants to complete. Headquarters may describe an API; the local requirement may be avoiding duplicate entry between two named systems. Keep those distinctions visible instead of treating the feature label as the answer.

Use one row per requirement, with a response and evidence for each vendor, followed by unresolved buyer-side questions. The structure might be:

| Buyer requirement | Our response and conditions | Competitor response and evidence | Buyer-side question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transfer orders without duplicate entry | Conditional: specify connection work and quotation scope | Record only verified documentation or vendor answers | Which system and team own the connection? |
| Restrict sensitive records to the relevant team | Confirm the proposed permission configuration | Leave unverified details explicitly unverified | Who needs access to which records? |

These are hypothetical examples, not assessments of real products. “Meets,” “meets with conditions” and “does not meet” can summarise a response, but they cannot replace its evidence. Do not fill an unknown competitor condition with a negative rating.

For an overseas vendor, identify where headquarters’ standard offer ends and local implementation work begins. If a Japanese interface, document format or local support arrangement is required, confirm the actual requirement and delivery scope. Do not assume those needs merely because the customer is in Japan.

## Explain limitations without manufacturing weaknesses

If a requirement cannot be met, say so before the buyer relies on it. Separate a supported configuration, additional implementation work and an unsupported request. A workaround is not the same promise as a standard feature.

For example, if integration needs paid development, state who provides it and which quotation includes it. Otherwise, a feature comparison can conceal work that the customer will have to fund or manage. Conversely, there is no reason to invent a limitation simply to make the sheet look balanced.

Ask the customer who will read the comparison next and what that person needs to decide. Their answer may point to security, procurement, finance or the operating team; it should come from the actual deal. This helps determine which unanswered conditions matter, without assuming an unseen approval committee.

## Share the version needed for the current decision

Do not withhold all comparison information until the final shortlist. If a mandatory requirement determines eligibility, give an accurate answer early. Add implementation conditions and commercial assumptions as the requirement becomes clearer.

When naming competing products, identify the product, plan and date of verification. If only public documentation is available, say where vendor confirmation is still needed. Japanese-language material should preserve those qualifications rather than turn a conditional answer into an unconditional claim.

**A useful comparison lets the customer explain both fit and the obligations that come with it. Keep the feature comparison, but connect each answer to a real requirement, verified evidence and implementation conditions.**
