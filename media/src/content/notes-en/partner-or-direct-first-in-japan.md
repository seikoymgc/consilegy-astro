The Japan entry decision usually arrives at HQ as a channel question. Sign a distributor and get to revenue in one quarter, or hire a country manager and build direct over three. Finance prefers the first. The regional lead prefers the second.

Both framings share the same blind spot. Whichever route you take, the thing that decides whether year two works is not how fast the first deal closes. It is whether the reason the other nine deals stalled ends up in a system you own.

Partner-led entry is usually faster. It is also the route where that reason never arrives.

## What foreign-affiliated companies in Japan actually partner with is not a distributor

JETRO, the Japanese government's trade and investment organisation, surveys foreign-affiliated companies operating in Japan every year. The 2024 edition (fieldwork 15 October to 17 November 2024, published 25 March 2025, 7,301 companies contacted, 1,427 valid responses, 19.5% response rate) covers all foreign-affiliated firms in Japan, not only software companies, so read the manufacturing and trading weight into it.

32.2% of respondents were running or considering a collaboration with a domestic Japanese company, university or research institute (n=1,380: 20.7% running one, 11.5% considering). In the information and communications sector the figure is 42.4% (25.0% running, 17.4% considering, n=144).

The counterparty breakdown is the part HQ does not expect (n=522, multiple response):

- Large Japanese enterprises, excluding distributors: 45.0%
- Japanese SMEs, excluding distributors and startups: 42.7%
- Universities: 38.5%
- **Distributors and sales agents: 25.9%**

Distributors are fourth. In services alone, large enterprises rise to 55.1% and distributors fall to 20.3% (n=187).

The most common content of those collaborations is joint marketing and channel development for the Japanese market, at 40.5% overall and 58.9% in information and communications (n=519, multiple response).

Your global partner programme is built around a motion where somebody resells your licence and implements it for a margin. In Japan, the arrangement that shows up most often in the data is a commercial partner who opens the market with you, not one who resells you. If your Japan plan starts by asking which distributor to appoint, you have narrowed the field before you have looked at it.

One caveat on a number HQ will want to quote. Among companies running a collaboration, 57.0% grew revenue year on year (n=270), against 52.0% for those considering one (n=150) and 45.9% for those doing neither (n=796), with the full sample at 48.7%. That is a correlation. Companies already growing find it easier to attract Japanese counterparties, and the same figures support that reading equally well.

## Your global partner list is the third door in Japan, not the first

The default HQ plan is to extend an existing global alliance into the region. The same survey asked how companies found their Japanese counterparties (n=507, multiple response):

- Attending domestic trade shows and events in Japan: 68.8%
- Referral from an existing partner company: 49.1%
- **Referral through the overseas parent company: 28.0%**

The parent company route is third, at well under half the rate of domestic events. In services, referral from an existing partner rises to 54.6% while the parent route reaches 30.6% (n=183).

A global systems integrator with a Japan entity does not necessarily have buyers in your category in Japan. Plan the entry on the assumption that the parent's introduction is a supplement, not the pipeline, and budget for domestic event presence in the first year.

## The direct route is gated before the first deal, by hiring

The alternative is not cheap either, and its constraint sits upstream of any pipeline.

In the same survey, 42.4% of respondents said they were finding it hard to secure the people they need (n=1,385). Among those, sales and marketing talent was the hardest category at 57.3%, ahead of IT and technical talent at 39.1% (n=583, multiple response). In the major metropolitan prefectures, Tokyo, Kanagawa, Aichi and Osaka, sales and marketing rises to 61.7% (n=472).

"Go direct" is, in practice, "start after you have hired a Japanese seller." The sequencing consequences are covered in [In Japan, your first bottleneck isn't translation or price. It's hiring.](/en/articles/japan-entry-bottleneck-is-hiring/)

## Decide on the record, not the channel

Neither set of numbers tells you which route is correct. They tell you that both routes have a constraint, and that the constraint is not the one on the slide.

Change the criterion. For the first ten Japanese deals, ask whether these three things land in a system you own.

**1. Where the deal stopped, and who did not agree.** Japanese purchases clear multiple approval stages. If you cannot see which stage a deal reached before it went quiet, you cannot fix the deck, the pricing sheet or the security answers. When a partner owns the account, that information stays in an account manager's head and never crosses into your region reporting. HQ then reads the aggregate and concludes that Japan is slow, which is a description, not a cause.

**2. The loss reason, at the granularity the buyer used.** Partner-reported losses compress into "budget" and "timing" almost every time. A quarter of that reporting tells you nothing you can act on. Japanese buyers drop vendors for reasons that are specific and fixable, and none of them survive that compression.

**3. One counting rule for partner-registered and direct deals.** If partner deal registration and your own opportunities are not reconciled from day one, the same Japanese company appears twice, regional pipeline inflates, and nobody can say which route produced the revenue. This is the failure that shows up latest and costs most, because the numbers look fine until the forecast misses.

If all three land, start with a partner and take the speed. If none of them land, the partner route will produce revenue and zero market knowledge, and you will enter year two with a bigger number and the same understanding you had at the start.

The implementation is small. Add eight fields before the first partner deal, not after: referral source, referring individual, referral type, referral date, commission eligibility, partner-side owner, status, and the linked opportunity. Then make the loss reason and the approval stage reached a contractual deliverable in the partner agreement rather than a favour you ask for quarterly.

One more reason to treat your own first ten deals as the dataset: there is no published Japanese primary data on the success or exit rate of foreign software companies entering Japan. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry discontinued its survey of foreign-affiliated companies after fiscal 2020, and nothing has replaced it. Benchmarking this decision against peers is not available to you.

**Decide Japan's entry route not by which one produces revenue first, but by which one puts the reason a deal stalled into your own record.**

Pull three currently open partner-sourced Japanese deals and read the last recorded loss or stall reason. If all three say budget or timing, that channel is sending you revenue and no information, and the second year of your Japan plan is being written blind.
