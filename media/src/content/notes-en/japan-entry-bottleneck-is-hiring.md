HQ usually opens the Japan launch with two debates: Japanese-language content and local pricing. Both are necessary.

But a prospect who finds your translated site still needs someone to pitch in Japanese, help push the internal approval through, and support them after go-live. Without that person, deals do not move.

That person is the role foreign companies in Japan struggle most to secure. Put hiring at the back of the plan, and the localization is finished on a day when nobody is there to operate it.

## The hardest role for foreign companies to fill in Japan is sales and marketing

JETRO, the Japanese government's trade and investment organisation, surveys foreign-affiliated companies operating in Japan every year. In the FY2024 edition (fieldwork 15 October to 17 November 2024, published 25 March 2025, 7,301 companies contacted, 1,427 valid responses, 19.5% response rate), 42.4% of respondents said they were finding it hard to secure the people they need (n=1,385).

Among those, sales and marketing talent was the hardest category at 57.3%, ahead of IT and technical talent at 39.1% (n=583, multiple response). In the major metropolitan prefectures, Tokyo, Kanagawa, Aichi and Osaka, sales and marketing rises to 61.7% (n=472).

The sample covers all foreign-affiliated firms in Japan, including manufacturing and trading, not only software companies. It is still the most defensible data point on this question: a government survey with a disclosed sample and response rate.

What the survey answers is which roles are hard to secure. It does not tell you how many months a hire takes, what compensation closes one, or how much a missing hire delays a launch.

## The first Japan sales hire does a job your HQ job description does not describe

Because this is the hardest role to fill, rewrite what the role actually is for Japan before you post it.

In Japanese B2B buying, a champion saying "this is good" is where the work begins: multi-department consensus and multi-stage internal approval follow. The first hire needs less the ability to present the product than the ability to move that agreement forward inside the buyer. At minimum, the job description should include these four responsibilities.

- Map the departments and roles involved, and track who has not yet said yes.
- Hand the buyer's contact a draft of the ringi (formal circulated approval) document and a case study from a similar-sized company in the same industry.
- Record in the CRM which approval stage each deal is stuck at.
- Keep loss reasons and stall points in your own records, including for partner-sourced deals.

Recruit against an English-market sales profile and you miss the people who can carry this. Japanese fluency is one requirement, not the center of the role.

## What to have ready before the first hire arrives

Compensation and time-to-hire are the first numbers your plan needs. This note cannot give you a market range backed by published primary data.

Do not plug in HQ's sense of the market or another country's benchmark. Take the rewritten job description to Japanese hiring channels (recruitment agencies, referrals) and use what comes back as the planning assumption. If you send the English HQ description instead, the ranges you get back describe a different job.

On the receiving side, have two things ready.

- **A CRM record structure.** Alongside the sales stage, give each deal an "approval stage" and the date it entered that stage. What your first hire learns in the first handful of deals does not reach HQ unless it lands here.
- **Something to hand over in the first meeting.** Japanese-language material and a case study from a similar-sized company in the same industry. If you have no Japanese customers yet, decide before the hire arrives what you will present as precedent.

## Covering the gap with outside capacity

Until hires land, the gap gets covered by partners, contractors, or people flying in from HQ. This note cannot point you to public data on which of those works best.

The same JETRO survey does show that foreign companies running a collaboration with Japanese companies or universities had a higher share of revenue growth (57.0% of those collaborating, against 45.9% of those neither collaborating nor considering it). But the counterparties are mostly large companies, SMEs and universities, and the most common content is joint marketing and channel development. It is not evidence that outsourcing your sales function works.

It is also a correlation: companies already growing find it easier to attract partners, and the same figures support that reading equally well.

The axis you can actually decide on is whether the record of each deal stays with you, whichever option you pick. Who objected, which stage it stalled at, why it was lost. If the period you hand to outsiders leaves no record in your own system, your first hire inherits nothing. **In a Japan launch, hiring is not a step that follows translation and pricing. The local sales and marketing people who will run the market are the role foreign companies struggle most to secure, so rewrite the role for Japan and prepare to receive the hire at the same time you start localizing.**

## Common mistakes

- **Hiring last.** Finishing localization and pricing before you start recruiting pushes the window where no one is operating the market further back. Rewriting the job description can start the same week as translation.
- **Reusing the HQ job description.** Recruiting against an English-market sales profile misses the people who can carry consensus building and internal approval, and the compensation ranges you get back describe the wrong job.
- **Settling for "speaks Japanese."** The requirement is not language; it is the ability to move a deal to close inside Japanese B2B norms. Miss that, and the hire will not move deals.
