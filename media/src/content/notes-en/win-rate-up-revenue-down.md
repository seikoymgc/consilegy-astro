Japan's win rate moved from 18% to 26% and Japan revenue came in under last quarter. Headquarters reads the first number as the local team finally landing the playbook, and the second as a timing problem.

Both readings are usually wrong, and for the same reason. Win rate is a ratio. It does not describe money.

Revenue is deal count multiplied by win rate multiplied by average deal size. A regional report that surfaces one of the three has hidden the other two.

## The arithmetic before the Japan part

If win rate improves from 18% to 26% while closed deal count falls from 120 to 70 in the same period, closed-won drops from about 21.6 to about 18.2. If average deal size fell too, revenue falls further. Every number in that sentence is accurate. Only the conclusion drawn from the first one is not.

There are three ways a win rate rises, and only one of them is performance.

**Hard deals moved out of the period.** Anything pushed past the close date leaves the denominator. The hardest deals get pushed first, so what remains looks better.

**Small deals took a bigger share.** Fewer approval layers, faster cycle, higher win rate, lower average deal size.

**The qualification bar went up.** Fewer opportunities created, better ones, higher win rate. This is the only one you would want to fund more of.

On a dashboard the three are identical. Deal count and average deal size are what separate them.

## Why the first one is the default outcome in Japan

In many regions a dead deal gets marked closed-lost. In Japan it more often stays open. The buyer does not decline, the internal approval simply does not move, and nobody involved wants to declare it finished while a relationship exists.

That habit removes deals from the denominator without removing them from the pipeline. A Japan win rate calculated from your global CRM is therefore structurally flattered relative to regions where losses get recorded promptly, and the size of the flattery depends on local hygiene rather than on local performance.

Ebsta and Pavilion's 2024 B2B Sales Benchmarks (published February 2024, 4.2 million opportunities across 530 companies, an international study rather than a Japanese one) found 44% of deals slipped past their close date, and that slipped deals won 67% less often. Their 2025 GTM Benchmarks (655,000 opportunities, 387 companies, also international) put the realised win rate at 13% for deals that slipped a month, 8% at three months, and 3% beyond six.

A deal pushed out of this quarter does not come back and lift next quarter's win rate. It lands in next quarter's denominator carrying a low probability. The improvement you booked this quarter is repaid with interest in the next one.

## In Japan, this quarter's win rate is not about this quarter

The second structural issue is cohort lag, and Japan has more of it than most regions you compare it against.

A joint study by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published April 2026, n=307, covering people who took part with at least one colleague in evaluating B2B products with an annual or one-off value above 3 million yen; a research-PR study limited to larger, multi-stakeholder purchases) found 63.6% of decisions took three to eight months from start to vendor selection, with roughly 40% taking six months or more. Approval ran to two stages for 60.9% of respondents and to two or more stages for over 80%.

The deals that closed in Japan this quarter were created two quarters ago, under the enablement, pricing, and messaging you had then. When headquarters credits this quarter's win rate to this quarter's regional initiative, the wrong initiative gets funded and the one that actually worked gets cut before its results arrive.

## Do not compare Japan's win rate to another region's

There is no published primary benchmark for Japanese lead-to-opportunity or opportunity-to-win conversion. We looked; the large datasets are international, and they mix deal sizes and approval structures that Japan does not share.

That has a practical consequence. A cross-region win rate column in the global dashboard invites a comparison that the underlying data cannot support. Japan's number can only be read against Japan's own history, on a consistent cohort.

If you keep the column, keep deal count and average deal size beside it, and stop ranking regions on the ratio alone.

## What headquarters should ask for instead

- **Four columns before the ratio.** Closed deal count, won count, won value, and average deal size, with win rate as a calculated fifth column. Put the ratio first and the ratio is all anyone reads.
- **A count of close-date changes.** One column showing how many Japan deals had their close date moved in the period tells you whether the win rate rose because the team improved or because the denominator emptied.
- **A written rule for marking a Japan deal lost.** Without one, Japan's denominator is decided by politeness. A simple rule works: no buyer-side activity and no approval movement for 60 days closes the deal, and it can be reopened if it returns.
- **Median as well as mean deal size.** A single large win moves the average and hides a shift toward small deals underneath it.
