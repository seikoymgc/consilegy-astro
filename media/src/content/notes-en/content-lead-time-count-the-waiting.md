Your Japan team took three weeks to publish one Japanese case study. The QBR question is whether the team needs another content hire, or a translation agency on retainer.

Before answering, open the timeline of that case study. Suppose the writing took three days.

The rest was waiting: for the HQ web team's sprint, for a global legal review of text no one in legal could read, for the translation vendor's second round.

**In that case, a faster writer does not shorten a single one of the waiting days.** The number to track is days waited, not days worked.

## Content that isn't live misses the shortlist

The reason this matters is on the buyer's side.

One Marketing surveyed 600 executives, directors and purchasing/contract staff across Japan between September 30 and October 1, 2025. At the point of their first meeting with a vendor's salesperson, 29% said the decision was "nearly made" and 56% said it was "narrowed to a few candidates."

That is 85% ("B2B Purchasing Process White Paper 2025," published November 2025).

The survey tells you that most of these buyers had narrowed the field before the first sales meeting.

It does not tell you that publishing a few days earlier changes win rates. What follows is inference: in Japan, that early research happens in Japanese, so the Japanese-language material you have live is what gets read.

Timing matters most for material tied to a date: a pricing change, case studies meant to be up before a trade show, a response to a regulatory change.

While those are stuck in a queue, your Japan reps explain the same thing again in every first meeting. A draft that is approved in principle but not published does not exist for the buyer.

## Three weeks of lead time is rarely three weeks of work

When lead time is reported as one number, every proposed fix is about producing faster: more writers, AI drafts, a translation agency, better templates.

All of those shorten working days. None of them touch waiting days.

For a Japan team inside a global company, the waits often sit at the handoffs with HQ.

A small Japanese text change may have to go through a central web team in another time zone. Brand review can approve layouts but not check the Japanese copy, so the page bounces back for a back-translation. Legal may apply the global review process to every page, including a corrected typo.

None of these appear in anyone's performance metrics, because nobody is idle while a piece waits.

## Record three things per piece

No new tooling is needed. For each published piece, write down:

- Days from brief to publish
- How many of those days it was waiting
- Who or what it was waiting on

The third column is the one that matters. "Approvals are slow" leads to another meeting where everyone agrees to move faster. "Waiting for the HQ web team's sprint" tells you exactly whose calendar to get on.

Fix the counting rule first.

Start is the day the request ticket was created (or the brief was approved), end is the publish date, count business days. A waiting day is any day the piece sat with someone other than the Japan content owner; if it waited on two parties at once, count it once.

Take the dates from records, not memory: status history in your task tool, the email or Slack message asking for review and the reply, the CMS revision history. From memory, the loudest person's explanation becomes the finding.

Compare at least three pieces of the same type, case studies with case studies. Different content types go through different review paths, so their waits will scatter for that reason alone.

## When the waits cluster, fix that handoff first

If three pieces stalled at the same place, that is where to start. Most organizations have more than one source of waiting; you do not need to fix them all at once.

Last year, at a global brand (consumer, not B2B), one campaign page took several weeks to go live. Content was not the problem. Even a minor copy change required a development cycle.

After the CMS, templates and workflow were rebuilt so that marketers could publish themselves, brief-to-publish lead time fell by roughly 70 to 80%. Pages now go live the same day or within a few business days.

That was not a Japan case.

But copy changes queuing behind development work is a common pattern on B2B websites too, including the Japanese pages of global sites. When the waits concentrate in one place, removing that one step from the path shortens lead time in one go.

One option for a Japan office is a local publishing lane: Japan can publish text and image changes within HQ-approved templates on the global CMS, while templates, URLs and embedded forms stay locked.

Changing a URL or a form can break marketing automation triggers, redirects, links already sent in email or ads, and page-level reporting, so those changes still go through whoever owns marketing operations.

## Don't remove review. Decide in advance what needs it

If the wait is legal or management review, removing review is the wrong fix. A Japanese page with pricing logic or competitor comparisons published without review will cost more later than it saved.

Change the scope instead. Agree, on one page, which kinds of changes Japan can publish without HQ sign-off and which always need it (pricing, competitor comparisons, customer names, quantified claims).

A corrected typo and a rewritten pricing explanation should not be in the same queue.

Marketing drafts that page; the reviewers (legal, the business owner) sign it.

A scope marketing set alone tends to collapse back into full review after the first incident. Back it with CMS permissions, for example by limiting who can publish, so the rule holds in the system and not only on paper. Edits to pages already live follow the same rule.

Reviewers return the things that do need them faster when they stop receiving everything.

## What the budget owner decides depends on who the piece waited on

If the waits cluster on development, fund CMS and template work rather than another writer. If they cluster on legal or management review, ask for the review-scope page, signed by the reviewers. If they cluster on an agency, revisit turnaround terms and revision rounds in the contract.

Approve headcount when waits are low and the working days themselves are the bottleneck.

## Run it on your last three Japanese pieces

Pick the last three Japanese-language pieces of the same type your team published and fill in the three columns. If the dates are already in your task tool and email, it takes about fifteen minutes.

If they stalled at the same place, you have what you need for the conversation with that team. If they did not, either add more pieces or you are not yet capturing waits reliably.

In that case, do not add a new field. Add one status to the task tool you already use, such as "Waiting: [who]," so the timestamp is recorded automatically. Asking people to log days by hand tends to stop by the third piece.

It is unglamorous work. It is also the part of the content operation that decides whether your Japanese material is live while buyers are still choosing.
