A Japan deal agrees on price and scope. You send the standard agreement. Then it goes quiet.

Nobody says no. The champion keeps replying to unrelated threads and says legal is reviewing.

Your CRM records "in legal review" and the deal crosses into the next quarter.

## Legal is already in the room while you are still selling

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa of Demagen Soken (published 9 June 2026, fielded 25 to 26 May 2026, n=330, restricted to people who took part with at least one colleague in Japanese purchases of B2B business software, SaaS, IT infrastructure or security with annual contract value of 5 million yen or more; an online survey run by a firm whose business is survey-based PR, so read it for order of magnitude) asked which specialist functions took part in the evaluation.

Legal appeared in 33.2% of evaluations, behind IT at 42.2% and finance at 38.2% (multiple response, n=325).

In roughly one deal in three, legal has been looking at you before your contract ever arrives.

That reorders the sequence most foreign vendors assume. Legal review is not the administrative tail of a closed deal. It is running in parallel with your sales process, somewhere you cannot see.

## The clause is rarely what stalls the deal. The missing owner is.

Japanese counsel returns a redline. Your Japan lead forwards it to headquarters.

From that point nobody owns the clock. There is a time-zone gap, the comments have to be rewritten in English, and the request joins a queue behind every other region. Two weeks later your Japan lead can still only say that headquarters is reviewing it.

For the buyer's legal team, an unanswered point is heavier than an unfavourable one. Internal policy leaves unconfirmed items marked open, and an approval document submitted with open items makes the person who filed it personally accountable for them.

So the champion waits rather than files. From your side, that looks like a deal that stopped caring.

## Japanese counsel marks up the same five places nearly every time

Governing law and jurisdiction. Auto-renewal and the notice period for termination. Limitation of liability. Handling of personal data and the scope of subprocessing. And whether the agreement contains an anti-organised-crime clause, which most Japanese companies treat as mandatory and which almost no foreign template includes.

None of this is Japanese exceptionalism. It is that contract review runs off an internal checklist, and a blank line on that checklist is not something the reviewer is allowed to skip.

Which means the stall is predictable. If it is predictable, you can answer it before the redline arrives.

## Send a position sheet before you send the contract

One page, clause by clause: what you can concede, what you can concede with conditions, and what you cannot move. For each immovable item, one line of reasoning and one alternative you can offer instead. Against every item, the name of the person at headquarters who answers it and how many working days that answer normally takes.

The buyer's legal team is not trying to establish whether your terms are reasonable. They are trying to establish how many days this will take.

Two approaches are worth naming and rejecting.

Translating the agreement into Japanese makes it readable and removes no points of contention. It adds one, because now somebody has to decide which language version governs.

Rewriting the whole agreement under Japanese law will not clear your own legal team, and forcing it through takes months. In practice a short Japan-specific addendum covers what the checklist needs.

## Once it is a stage, "waiting on legal" becomes a date

The CRM side is simple. Put four fields on the deal: legal review status, security review status, review owner, review due date.

Then two rules. A deal with legal or security review open cannot enter the contracting stage. A review past its due date notifies the sales manager.

After that, "we're waiting on legal" stops being a rep's impression and becomes a number of days overdue. Whether to hold the deal in the forecast becomes a decision you can make from the record rather than from the call.

**Legal review in Japan happens during the evaluation, so treat it as a deal stage rather than paperwork.**

## Common mistakes

- **Treating legal review as a post-close step.** It runs during the evaluation. Scheduling it after verbal agreement guarantees the slip you are trying to avoid.
- **Sending the master agreement with no position sheet.** The redline that comes back is the buyer guessing at what you will move on, which is a slower way to have the same conversation.
- **Leaving the turnaround unstated.** Even a slow answer with a committed date lets the champion keep filing. An open-ended one does not.
- **Assuming your template is complete because it clears other markets.** The anti-organised-crime clause is absent from nearly every foreign template and required by nearly every Japanese reviewer.
