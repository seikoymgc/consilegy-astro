Japan pipeline is flat, traffic is up, and someone at headquarters concludes that the brand is simply unknown in the market. A localisation budget and an awareness campaign follow.

Before approving it, ask the Japan team for one number: how many deals in the last quarter reached the buyer's shortlist and were cut from it.

Almost no Japan team can produce that number, which is exactly why the money keeps going to reach.

## Two losses, one word

"We lack awareness in Japan" collapses two completely different failures into one sentence.

In the first, the buyer never put you on the list. That is a reach problem.

In the second, the buyer put you on the list, compared you against two or three others, and removed you. That is a resolution problem, and no amount of impressions moves it.

The two need different owners, different budgets, and different content. Once they are merged under "awareness," the next decision always tilts toward spend, because spend is the only lever the word implies.

## In Japan, the second loss is invisible to your reps

A joint survey by IDEATECH and Hiroyasu Kitagawa (published 16 April 2026, fielded 23 to 26 March 2026, n=307, limited to respondents who took part with at least one colleague in Japanese B2B purchases of 3 million yen or more; a vendor-sponsored survey skewed toward larger, multi-stakeholder deals) found that by the time serious vendor conversations begin, the buying process is on average about 40% complete.

Roughly 90% of those buyers had already shortlisted several vendors before engaging a rep, and in about 70% of cases the final comparison came down to two or three vendors.

So a Japanese buyer who shortlisted you, evaluated you quietly, and dropped you never appears in your CRM. Your local rep never spoke to them. Your headquarters dashboard reads that as absence of demand and funds awareness against it.

This is worse for a foreign vendor than for a domestic one. The Japanese buyer is comparing you against local incumbents whose materials answer the two questions below without being asked.

## What actually gets you cut, and what gets you picked

In the same survey, the top reasons buyers removed a vendor from the list were missing functionality at 39.1%, company size not being a fit at 34.5%, and no case study from a similar company at 26.4% (multiple response).

Among respondents who had chosen a vendor and narrowed to a final shortlist (this question only, n=298), the top reason for selection was "there was a case study from a company of similar size" at 44.3%, ahead of clear functionality at 40.3% and a track record in the industry at 29.5%.

"Your size does not fit us" and "there is no company like ours in your cases" are not reach problems. They are questions your Japanese page does not answer, and an awareness campaign will simply deliver more people to the same unanswered page.

## The sequence

First, split the last quarter of Japanese losses and non-responses into the two boxes. The practical test is whether the buyer named you first or you named yourself. That separates reach from resolution well enough to act on.

Second, if the resolution box is larger, rebuild the Japanese case library by company size crossed with industry, not by industry alone. In the same survey, industry-specific information was decisive for 16.9% of buyers and reinforced the decision for a further 45.6%, so 62.5% were influenced by it. A translated global logo wall does not carry this. A Japanese customer of roughly the buyer's headcount does.

Third, only if the reach box is larger do you fund reach, and not into the channels headquarters is used to. The same survey found 91.2% of buyers used generative AI or AI search during evaluation, with comparison at 47.6% and price research at 46.9% as the leading uses. The place where Japanese shortlists get assembled has moved; the media plan usually has not.

This is the same work as getting the [Japan GTM and messaging](https://consilegy.com/en/services-en/japan-market-gtm-messaging/) right, because the case library and the positioning have to be built from Japanese deals rather than translated from the global site.

## Common mistakes

- **Funding awareness because Japan pipeline is flat.** Flat pipeline is consistent with both failures. Split the losses before you size the budget, or you will spend a year sending more buyers to a page that already lost them.
- **Translating the global case studies and calling it localisation.** The Japanese buyer is checking for a company of their size, in their industry. A translated enterprise US logo answers neither question.
- **Treating "we had never heard of you" as proof of low awareness.** In Japan it is also a polite way to close a conversation. Weigh what the buyer did, not what they said at the end.
