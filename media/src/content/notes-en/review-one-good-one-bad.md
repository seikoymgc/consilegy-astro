The monthly Japan review runs on schedule. The slides are clean, everyone has seen the figures, and the next month starts exactly like the last one.

The usual diagnosis is that the team is passive or the data is thin. More often **the meeting opens with the whole table, so nobody ever gets to the reasons.** We changed our own monthly review to open single cases, and the "so what?" ending stopped.

## A table invites confirmation, not explanation

Put last month's results on screen and everyone nods at the same figures. Agreement is easy at that level. The reasons are not on the screen, though, so someone has to volunteer a hypothesis, and in a Japanese team that is often a senior person who would rather not guess in public. If no one does, the meeting runs out on confirmation.

Survey data on how review screens are used points the same way. In a survey by Keywalker (fielded in October 2025, n=1,034 sales managers and field staff at Japanese companies that had adopted SFA, CRM or BI tools, online panel), 58.2% of managers used the dashboard to check figures in sales meetings, against 15.4% who used it to decide the next action. The sponsor sells BI implementation support, so read the percentages as orders of magnitude.

## One win and one miss give the room something to talk about

Each month we review the previous month's LinkedIn posts. The format is simple. In the first five minutes the group scans all the figures, with no discussion. Then ten minutes on one post that did well, and ten minutes on one that did poorly. The last five minutes decide what changes next month.

A single case is concrete. Nobody has an opinion on "the average moved", but everyone has something to say about why one post was read: the topic, the first line, the timing, the length. The reasons that were invisible in the table appear once one item is open.

The questions are fixed. For the strong case: why did it work, and can the pattern be repeated? For the weak case: why was the response low, and what would fix it? Without the second question in each pair, the meeting turns into commentary.

## Leave with one or two changes

The output is one or two changes, at the level of "use more story-led posts" or "shorten the headline". More than two and nobody can tell next month which one worked. The last two minutes are a read-back of the strong case, the case to fix, and the change. The next meeting opens by checking that change first.

For a Japan team reporting to HQ, this also gives the team a position it can defend. A change tied to one named case is easier to approve than a general promise to "improve engagement".

## How deep the review goes tracks results, with caveats

A related pattern shows up in sales and marketing alignment. In the "BtoB Marketing Survey 2026" by Fast Marketing, Inc. (fielded May 2026, n=432 sales and marketing staff at B2B companies in Japan, online panel), one question asked how far results are shared back after leads are handed to sales (Q18). Companies that shared the reasons for wins and losses reported 82.1% attainment of their win target; companies that shared only whether the lead was followed up reported 54.3%.

Two cautions. The publisher sells B2B marketing strategy support, and the report itself says the figures show correlation, not causation. Companies that are already doing well may simply have more room to discuss reasons. Still, seeing a result and explaining it are different acts, and the survey is consistent with the second being the one that matters.

## Applying it to your next Japan review

Do not open the table first. Before the meeting, pick one strong case and one weak case, using the top and bottom figures if you have no better rule. Then check the end of the meeting: does it produce a named change for next month? A review that cannot name one has confirmed the numbers, which a document could have done without calling everyone in.
